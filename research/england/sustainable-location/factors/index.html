What decides a sustainable location: the factors in 247 decisions

Every factor inspectors and councils have used to decide whether a site is a sustainable location since the August 2026 Framework took effect, grouped into 11 classes and counted.

In short #

  • We went through every decision in the database where the sustainability of the location was assessed and recorded the factors the decision-maker used: 247 decisions, 205 of them appeals and 42 council decisions, made between 17 August 2026 and 2 October 2026.
  • They use 55 factors, which fall into 11 classes. Counting each factor once per decision gives 1,792 factor findings, about 7 a decision: 649 counted for the location, 898 against, and 245 were raised but set aside or neutral.
  • The walking route is what most often decides. It was among the decisive factors in 117 of the 247 decisions. Whether there is a footway (143 decisions) and whether the route is lit (122) are two of the three most-used factors.
  • Distance cuts evenly. It came up in 134 decisions, counting for the location in 58 and against it in 56.
  • Cycling and the settlement hierarchy are often raised and rarely decide. Cycling was a factor in 75 decisions and decisive in 5; the hierarchy and plan position in 120 and decisive in 13.

This is a sub-page of How to assess a sustainable location under the 2026 NPPF, which sets out the test in policy TR3 of the National Planning Policy Framework (NPPF) and the guidance on it. The counts come from our notes on each decision; the method and its limits are below.

What was counted #

We screened 309 decisions: every one in the decisions database with a finding on TR3 or on the policies that refer to it, a main issue about location or accessibility, or repeated wording about car dependence or access to services.

  • 247 assess the location with at least one stated factor. These are the base for every count on this page.
  • 15 only assert or concede the point, for example by listing a "sustainable location" among the benefits with no reasons.
  • 47 turned out not to be about accessibility: for example "location" meant a town-centre sequential test, or TR3 was cited only for highway safety.

Of the 247, 108 found the location sustainable, 98 found it not sustainable, and 37 were mixed: shortcomings were found but tolerated, or the site passed one policy limb and failed another. 4 reached no finding. 232 applied the August 2026 Framework. 12 were decided after it took effect but used the wording of the December 2024 Framework, which TR3 carries forward, and 3 cite neither.

Decisions is the number of the 247 in which a factor of that class came into play. For, Against and Set aside count the factor findings: the decision-maker treated the factor as supporting a sustainable location, as counting against it, or raised it and then rejected it, gave it little or no weight, or left it neutral. Decisive is the number of decisions in which a factor of that class was one of the one to three that settled the location finding.

What the totals show #

  • The walking route dominates. It has 508 findings, more than twice any other class, and 73% of them count against the location. It was decisive in 117 decisions; the next class, bus and road public transport, in 70.
  • Footway and lighting lead. Whether there is a footway was a factor in 143 decisions and decisive in 98; street lighting in 122 and decisive in 63. Neither is measured by the Connectivity Tool.
  • Distance cuts evenly (58 for, 56 against) and was decisive in 43 decisions, fewer than half as many as footway presence. A distance benchmark such as 800 metres was applied in 20.
  • Buses are raised in more decisions than anything else (173) and count for the location more often than against it (116 to 85). The exception is evidence: where the decision-maker had no timetable or frequency evidence (12 decisions), 11 found the location not sustainable.
  • Cycling and the settlement hierarchy are argued but rarely decide. Cycling was decisive in 5 of the 75 decisions that weighed it. A settlement's tier in the council's hierarchy came up in 80 and was decisive in 8; see Service Village Does Not Mean Sustainable.
  • Scale and the nature of the scheme are the usual route to a mixed finding. They appear in 27 of the 37 mixed findings and count for the location 62 times against 13. The scale of the scheme itself (37 decisions) is less one-sided: 19 for, 9 against, 9 set aside.
  • Electric vehicles and home working did not help. They were argued in 7 decisions and never counted for the location.
  • Usability for everyone only ever counts against. Whether the route works for children, disabled people or older people, and in the dark or in winter, was a factor in 36 decisions and never counted for the location.
  • Earlier decisions carry little. Comparator appeals and permissions were raised in 36 decisions and set aside in 24.
  • The Connectivity Tool is cited in few decisions but matters when it is. A score was a factor in 27 decisions (12 for, 9 against) and among the decisive factors in 16.
  • Mitigation is offered in few decisions and usually helps. New footways, crossings, bus contributions and the like came up in 30 decisions; 38 of the 51 findings counted for the location.

Method and limits #

Each decision's factors were coded from our case file for it, which is our note of the decision letter or council report, against a fixed codebook. Every coded factor was then checked against the text of the decision itself: the letter or report has to contain wording for that factor. The settlement-tier factor follows the separate, quote-checked register behind Service Village Does Not Mean Sustainable. The register, with one row per decision, is in the research repository.

  • The counts are floors. A factor our note left out is not counted, so a factor may have been used in more decisions than shown.
  • The text check is a check of presence. It shows the decision mentions the factor. It does not prove which way the factor cut.
  • The split between for, against and set aside is approximate at the margin. A shortcoming that was found and then tolerated can fairly be read as against or as set aside. The number of decisions in which a factor came into play is the firmer figure.
  • "Decisive" is our reading of what settled the location finding, not a quotation from the decision.
  • Two factors are probably undercounted: narrow or single-track lanes, and the Public Transport Accessibility Level (PTAL) used in London. Both were added after the first pass.
  • The location finding is not the outcome. A scheme in a location found sustainable can still be refused on other grounds, and the reverse.
