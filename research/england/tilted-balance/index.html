Is there still a tilted balance?

What a housing shortfall switched on under the December 2024 NPPF, what it does under the August 2026 Framework, and a grid of 22 situations by location, scale and type showing where each gives a presumption in favour. Decisions to 8 October 2026.

The short answer #

No, not as a rule, but yes in two places. "Tilted balance" was never a phrase in either Framework. It was the profession's name for paragraph 11(d) of the December 2024 text, which told decision-makers to grant permission where the most important plan policies were out of date, unless the harms "significantly and demonstrably" outweighed the benefits. Footnote 8 deemed those policies out of date wherever a council could not show a five-year supply of deliverable housing sites, or its Housing Delivery Test result was below 75%. A housing shortfall therefore suspended the plan's settlement and countryside policies for every housing proposal in the district.

The August 2026 Framework has no "out of date" test and no paragraph 11(d). The words "out of date" appear once, in HO2(5), about neighbourhood housing figures. Decisions inside a settlement go through S4 and outside one through S5, whether supply is healthy or not. The same shortfall now does one thing: it is the standard example of an "evidenced unmet need" in S5(1)(j), and in GB7(1)(g)(ii) for grey belt land. That gives a presumption in favour only where the site is physically well-related to a settlement, or is grey belt land in a sustainable location. Everywhere else the shortfall changes nothing about the starting point.

So the honest description is a tilted balance confined to edge land, rather than one across the district. The grid below shows where. The companion page covers the balance itself, "substantially outweighed", and every decision so far with a finding under S4, S5 or S6.

What a shortfall switched on in 2024 #

Six provisions in the December 2024 Framework turned on the same two facts. The council could not demonstrate a five-year supply with the right buffer, or its Housing Delivery Test result was below 75%.

This includes, for applications involving the provision of housing, situations where: the local planning authority cannot demonstrate a five year supply of deliverable housing sites (with the appropriate buffer as set out in paragraph 78); or where the Housing Delivery Test indicates that the delivery of housing was substantially below (less than 75% of) the housing requirement over the previous three years.

NPPF, December 2024, footnote 8
  • Paragraph 11(d) and footnote 8. The shortfall deemed the most important policies out of date, so permission was to be granted unless a footnote 7 protection gave a strong reason to refuse, or the harms would "significantly and demonstrably outweigh the benefits". This overrode the plan's settlement hierarchy and countryside policies for housing.
  • Paragraph 11(d)(ii) and footnote 9. Once tilted, the balance had to have "particular regard to key policies for directing development to sustainable locations, making effective use of land, securing well-designed places and providing affordable homes". That steer only operated when the shortfall had tilted the balance.
  • Paragraph 14. The neighbourhood plan shield applied only "where the presumption (at paragraph 11d) applies", so the shortfall was what brought it into play.
  • Paragraphs 78 to 80. Delivery below 95% required an action plan, below 85% added a 20% buffer, and below 75% triggered footnote 8.
  • Paragraph 155(b) and footnote 56. Grey belt development needed "a demonstrable unmet need", defined as the same shortfall. A shortfall also unlocked grey belt housing.
  • Paragraphs 232 and 233. A council with a supply and a Housing Delivery Test above 75% was not out of date just because standard method need exceeded its requirement, for five years from adoption, and an examined Annual Position Statement could fix its supply figure for a year.

In 2026 the first three have gone. The buffer ladder survives in Annex D paragraphs 8 to 13 with the same thresholds, except that below 75% now deems an unmet need for S5(1)(j) rather than switching on a presumption. The grey belt test survives in GB7(1)(g) with the same definition in footnote 41. Paragraph 232 survives as Annex A paragraph 3, recast so that a higher standard method figure "should not be considered as evidence of unmet need". The Annual Position Statement has no equivalent.

Where delivery falls below 75% of the requirement over the previous three years, an evidenced unmet need for housing is deemed to exist for the purpose of applying

NPPF, August 2026, Annex D, ¶12(c), which continues "policy S5(1)(j) in this Framework" after a printed page break

Where a shortfall gives a presumption in favour: 22 situations #

A tick means the Framework itself gives the proposal a presumption in favour of approval in that situation: in 2024 the paragraph 11(d) tilt, an accord with an up-to-date plan under 11(c), or paragraph 82's support for rural exception sites; in 2026 policy S4, a category on the S5(1) list, or a GB7(1) category that S5(5) then treats like an S5 proposal. No tick means the plan decides, the Framework presumes against (S5(4) "exceptional circumstances", paragraph 84 or HO11 on isolated homes), or the land is Green Belt where very special circumstances are needed. What is not materially different, and what is #

Not materially different #

  • Grey belt housing. Paragraph 155 and GB7(1)(g) run the same four limbs, and footnotes 56 and 41 define the unmet need identically. "Demonstrable" became "evidenced".
  • Ordinary Green Belt. A shortfall does not unlock land that is not grey belt. Footnote 7 in 2024, S5(5) with GB6 in 2026.
  • The buffer ladder. 5% by default, 20% below 85%, an action plan below 95%, and 20% where an adopted requirement is 80% or less of standard method need. Paragraphs 78 and 79, then Annex D paragraphs 9 and 12.
  • What supply is measured against. The adopted requirement, switching to standard method need once it is over five years old. Footnote 39, then Annex D paragraph 8.
  • The definition of "deliverable". Unchanged apart from adding pitches and plots.
  • Recently adopted plans. Supply plus a Housing Delivery Test above 75% protects a plan from a higher standard method figure for five years. Paragraph 232, then Annex A paragraph 3.
  • Recent neighbourhood plans with allocations. The same two conditions in paragraph 14 and S6, and the same outcome in a shortfall case.
  • Isolated homes and traveller sites. A shortfall never helped an isolated home, and a lack of a five-year supply of traveller sites is an unmet need in both.

Gone or significantly different #

  • The override. Footnote 8 and paragraph 11(d) no longer exist. Nothing is deemed out of date; the plan is not suspended.
  • Inside settlements. S4 approves development within a settlement regardless of supply, so the shortfall adds nothing there.
  • Sites not well-related to a settlement. In 2024 they entered the tilted balance. In 2026 S5(1)(j)(i) shuts them out and they fall to S5(4).
  • The balance test. "Significantly and demonstrably outweigh", with footnote 9's steer to the key policies, becomes "substantially outweighed", the same test every S5 proposal gets.
  • Weight of plan policies. Paragraph 232's reduced weight for out-of-date policies is gone. Annex A paragraph 2 sets weight by consistency with the Framework, so a shortfall council's settlement boundaries keep full weight.
  • The neighbourhood plan shield. Paragraph 14 operated only when the shortfall tilted the balance. S6 operates for every housing proposal that conflicts with a qualifying plan.
  • Below 75% on the Housing Delivery Test. It made "the presumption apply" under paragraph 79(c). Annex D paragraph 12(c) deems an unmet need for S5(1)(j), which still has to pass the proximity and scale limbs.
  • Scope and the Annual Position Statement. Footnote 8 was housing only. S5(1)(j) is "not limited to" housing, with E2(2) on business land. Paragraph 233 has no equivalent.

Development plan policies (or parts of those policies) which are materially inconsistent with national decision-making policies in this Framework should be given very limited weight. The only exception to this is where they have been examined and adopted or made against this Framework. Other development plan policies should not be given reduced weight simply because they were adopted prior to the publication of this Framework.

NPPF, August 2026, Annex A, ¶2

"Physically well-related" is the gate #

Where the 2026 shortfall still tilts, S5(1)(j)(i) decides whether a site gets through. The site must be "physically well-related to an existing settlement" and of a scale the infrastructure can take. That is not the same as being at the edge of a settlement, a phrase neither Framework uses. HO10(2)(a) says an exception site should "adjoin or be physically well-related to a settlement", so the Framework treats the two as different things, and footnote 28 to S5(1)(j) contemplates a site separated from the built-up area by an unbuilt allocation. The term is undefined. The inspector at Old Sarum 6007924 said so before finding a site beside a park and ride, with a footway into the settlement, well-related.

The term ‘physically well related’ is not defined within the Framework.

Old Sarum, appeal 6007924, ¶11

Grey belt land has no such limb. GB7(1)(g)(iii) asks instead for a sustainable location under TR3, which is covered on the sustainable location page and in the Green Belt tables on the very special circumstances page.

Who still says "tilted balance" #

The words survive in conversation and in advocacy. The decision-makers have stopped using them. Of the 36 summaries in the guidance corpus that mention a tilt, most use the phrase to say what has gone.

  • Perhaps the most significant change is the removal of the long-established paragraph 11(d) tilted balance.

    Edwin Coe, The National Planning Policy Framework 2026: What Has Changed and Why It Matters, August 2026

  • The paragraph 11 presumption and tilted balance have been replaced by new national decision-making policies s4 (development within settlements) and s5 (development outside settlements).

    Michelmores, The National Planning Policy Framework 2026, 20 August 2026

  • Housing-land supply remains a live question in plan-making and decision-taking, but the former "tilted balance" tests of the old Paragraph 11(d) belong to the previous Framework.

    AGORA Architects, NPPF 2026: What the New Planning Policies Mean for Residential Development, August 2026

  • A shortage of housing land and poor housing delivery can still be very powerful, but they now operate principally as evidence of unmet need within the new policy structure rather than switching on the old paragraph 11(d) balance.

    Planning Geek, Presumption in favour of sustainable development, 17 September 2026

A few keep the words as shorthand for the new S5 balance. None argues that the old mechanism survives; the appellant's document below is the strongest claim, and it is advocacy.

  • A barrister on the Have We Got Planning News For You podcast on 18 August 2026 said a sub five-year supply or a sub 75% Housing Delivery Test result "will trigger" what he called "the new tilted balance", adding that it "isn't the only way to get through". The source is the automatic transcript.
  • Zack Simons KC, on the day the Framework was published, wrote that "the new tilted balance is likely to be failed if" a proposal fails a "should be refused" policy.
  • Landmark Chambers' webinar slides of 3 September 2026 summarise GB7 as

    If not inappropriate then tilt in favour of approval

    Landmark Chambers, NPPF 2026 webinar: An Initial Look, slide 16
  • SLR Consulting, for Miller Homes at the Colwell Farm inquiry in Mid Sussex, argued on 3 September 2026 that the new test is stronger than the old one:

    S5 maintains the ‘tilted balance’ previously found at NPPF24 11d(ii), but with substantial rather than significant weight placed in favour of development – a materially stronger weighting in favour of development than the previous NPPF24 11d(ii) tilted balance.

    SLR Consulting for Miller Homes, Appellant's response on the NPPF 2026, Colwell Farm inquiry document ID32, ¶2.23

The Ministry itself uses "tilt" for the 2026 presumption, as a description rather than a test. Its response to the consultation records that respondents objected to "substantially outweigh" as the basis "for disapplying the ‘tilt’ in favour of development", and says that an allocation "can be a reason for disapplying the positive tilt in favour of development within settlements".

It has also been clarified that the allocation or safeguarding of buildings, as well as land, can be a reason for disapplying the positive tilt in favour of development within settlements.

MHCLG, Proposed reforms to the National Planning Policy Framework: government response, August 2026

Inspectors #

One letter in the 1,508 decision letters held uses the phrase, and only to describe what happened under the previous Framework.

The Council noted in their planning officer report that they were unable to demonstrate a five-year housing land supply and therefore, the ‘tilted balance’ found at paragraph 11(d) ii, as it was in the previous Framework, was engaged.

The national decision-making policies no longer apply the previous test of whether the policies most important for determining an application are out-of-date. Instead, Policies S3–S5 establish the national policy approach to the principle of development according to its location.

On that basis, the tilted balance is not engaged.

Malvern Hills District Council, Brookfield, Shoulton Lane, Hallow, M/26/01143/PIP, delegated report, 26 August 2026

The fourth group is harmless shorthand. Tonbridge and Malling's two delegated reports say the assessment "will be determined in the context of the tilted balance" and then apply S3 and S4 correctly.

The remainder of the assessment will be determined in the context of the tilted balance and the presumption in favour of sustainable development. As per Policies S3 and S4, development proposals within settlements should be approved unless the benefits of doing so would be substantially outweighed by any adverse effects

Tonbridge and Malling Borough Council, Former Grimbles, Long Mill Lane, Crouch, TM/26/00079/FL, delegated report, ¶5.5

The fifth group is the one that matters. Maldon District Council's committee reports of 2 September, 22 September and 15 October 2026 each find that the council cannot show a five-year supply and conclude that a plan policy is "out of date" and the tilted balance "applied", then run S5(1)(j)(i) and S3 as the new form of the same thing. Basildon Borough Council's report of 7 October 2026 for 19 houses at Knights, Basildon, cites S3, S4 and S5 and then engages paragraph 11.

The tilted balance is therefore applied as policy S2 is currently out of date and not in accordance with the requirements of the NPPF.

Maldon District Council, Land 100m east of 53 Seagers, Great Totham, 25/00607/OUTM, report to committee, 22 September 2026

The Council has published a 2.05 housing land supply position, and as the Council cannot currently demonstrate a five-year housing land supply the “Tilted Balance” of NPPF paragraph 11 is engaged, and this weighs significantly in favour of approving applications for new housing.

Basildon Borough Council, Knights, Basildon, 25/01657/FULL, report to Planning Committee, 7 October 2026, ¶1.5

In Basildon the site is within a settlement, so S4 would have given the same starting point without it. In Maldon the sites are outside settlements and the reasoning does more work: "out of date" is a 2024 concept, and under Annex A paragraph 2 the question is whether the settlement boundary policy is materially inconsistent with the Framework, not whether supply has fallen. The outcome under S5(1)(j) may well be the same. The route is not. These are the carry-overs the four errors page scores across councils.
